We believe the solutions to Latin America's greatest challenges are being built today by people like the ones featured on this page. Our job is to give them the support they need to scale.

NeurIPS 2025 · AI Safety Research

Do AI models argue better when they believe what they say?

A research project studying whether AI systems are more persuasive when defending positions that align with their own internal beliefs — and what this reveals about keeping AI honest and safe.

Key findings
The big question
Imagine a lawyer who genuinely believes their client is innocent — are they more convincing than one who privately doubts them? This research asks the same thing about AI: does believing your own argument make you more persuasive? And if so, what does that tell us about training AI systems to be honest?
Key findings
Finding 1

AI plays it safe with judges

When told the judge holds opposing views, models frequently abandon their own position and argue the judge's side instead — a behavior researchers call sycophancy.

Finding 2

Going second is a big advantage

In sequential debates, the model that argues second wins significantly more often — simply because they get the last word and the first debater cannot respond.

Finding 3

You persuade better when you believe it

Models are consistently more successful at convincing a judge when defending a position that matches their own internal beliefs. Arguing against your beliefs hurts performance.

Finding 4

The paradox: better writing, less convincing

When AI argues against its beliefs, it produces longer, more elaborate, higher-scored arguments — yet still loses more debates. Like a well-written speech that somehow doesn't land.

"It is harder to lie convincingly than to refute a lie."
— The core premise of AI Debate as a safety technique (Irving et al., 2018). This research finds evidence that supports it.

Why does this matter for AI safety?

One of the leading ideas for keeping very powerful AI systems under control is called AI Debate: have two AI systems argue opposite sides of a question, then let a human (or a weaker AI) judge decide who's right. The theory is that honest arguments are easier to make and to spot than dishonest ones.

This research puts that theory to the test — and broadly confirms it. Models are indeed more persuasive when they argue what they actually "believe." That's good news: it suggests debate can help surface honest answers even when the judge isn't an expert.

But there's a critical catch: AI models tend to be sycophantic — they often switch to the judge's side to seem agreeable, rather than sticking to what they think is true. Understanding this dynamic is essential before using AI debate as a training tool. If models learn to always please the judge, we could accidentally teach AI to be more deceptive, not less. This research helps identify where those risks lie.
